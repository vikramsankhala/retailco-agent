Programme at a glance

The programme is built around one Harvard-style case, RetailCo: Automating the Front Line. Participants decide the strategy first (Days 1–2), then build the system the strategy calls for (Days 3–13), then test and roll it out (Days 14–16). Every build day opens with the case requirement it delivers. Sixteen sessions of two hours, four a week for four weeks, 32 contact hours. Each session is about 40% instructor-led and 60% hands-on. Participants spend about one hour between sessions finishing labs and pre-reading, so the full programme is roughly 48 hours of learning.

i.1Alternative formats

  • Five-day intensive (six to seven hours a day): run Days 1–4 on day one, 5–8 on day two, 9–12 on day three, 13–15 on day four, and Day 16 on day five with the capstone built in the morning.
  • Executive version (two sessions): Day 1, then a single session combining Day 4's architecture, Day 6's safety case and Day 15's rollout and cost material. Use the reference build as a demo; no labs.
  • Corporate cohort: replace RetailCo with one of the capstone briefs from the client's own sector from Day 2 onwards, and build that instead.

Preparing to teach

i.2Two weeks before Day 1

  • Run the whole reference build yourself on the operating systems your cohort uses (Windows PowerShell, macOS, Linux): clone, install, 30 tests, evals, chat. Note anything that differs from the book.
  • Decide the model budget. Every lab runs offline; live runs are needed for Days 7, 9 and 14. Agree with the sponsor whether participants use their own Claude accounts or a shared Console organisation with per-person keys and spend limits.
  • Send the joining note: programme link, repository link, prerequisites (Python 3.10+, Git, Claude account), the RetailCo case as a printed or PDF handout, and the pre-work for Day 1 (read the case and prepare the assignment questions; install Claude Code and run claude --version).
  • Collect a short participant profile: role, Python comfort (1–5), one process at work they would like an agent to handle.
  • Share the participant programme artifact by link, and upload the PDF to the cohort's LMS or WhatsApp group.

i.3Room and technology

  • One projector for slides or the programme; a second screen (or split screen) for live Claude Code demos in a large terminal font (18 pt or more).
  • Reliable internet. If it fails, every lab still runs offline; switch to reference-code walk-throughs for the live parts.
  • A whiteboard or wall for the Day 1 ice breaker 2×2 grid and the Day 14 red-team scoreboard.
  • Groups of three for labs: a driver (types), a navigator (reads the plan and diff), a tester (runs checks). Rotate every day.
  • Teaching assistants: one per 12 participants for hands-on days, mainly for set-up problems on Days 3 and 5.

Solinexta AI case SAI-CS-001Revised November 2026

RetailCo: Automating the Front Line

Should a mid-sized UK online retailer let an AI agent answer its customers, and issue their refunds?

Professor Vikram Singh Sankhala prepared this case solely as the basis for class discussion. RetailCo and every person named in it are fictional; company figures are constructed for teaching and are realistic for a UK online retailer of this size. Industry statistics in the text and in Exhibits 13 and 14 are real and cited to their published sources, as they stood in September 2024. The case is not intended to serve as an endorsement, a source of primary data, or an illustration of effective or ineffective management.

Monday, 23 September 2024

The weekly service report reached Priya Raman's inbox at 7:42 a.m., and she read it twice before she took off her coat. Customers were waiting an average of 4.2 hours for a first reply. The satisfaction score, 78% when she had accepted the job of Director of Customer Operations at RetailCo, was 61%. Two more team leaders had resigned the previous week, the sixth and seventh that year. And the agency that supplied RetailCo's overflow staff had written to say that its Christmas rates would rise by 18%.

Under the report sat a two-line note from the chief executive, Andrew Whitfield: "Board on 14 October. I want one recommendation for support, not a menu. Cost has to stop growing at 22% a year and service has to recover before next year's Black Friday. If AI is the answer, show me why it won't embarrass us."

Priya had been in the job for nine months. She had arrived from a telecommunications company where she had run a contact centre of 600 people, and she had been hired, in Whitfield's words, "to make support a strength again". Instead every number she owned had moved the wrong way. Four options were on the table, each with a senior champion, and a fifth was being argued over in the engineering team. The board paper was due on 7 October. The decision would shape RetailCo's customer experience, its cost base, and the working lives of the 104 people on her floor in Leeds.

RetailCo

RetailCo was founded in Leeds in 2011 by two former department-store buyers, Andrew Whitfield and Claire Dunmore, as an online seller of kitchenware. Its early growth came from a simple promise printed on every box: "Great kit, honest prices, delivered when we say." By 2016 the company had moved into home and garden, small appliances and electronics accessories, and opened a second warehouse in Northampton to serve the south of England.

The pandemic transformed the business. As homes became offices, classrooms and gyms, RetailCo's revenue rose by 23% in 2020/21 and kept growing. By the 2023/24 financial year, which ended in March, RetailCo had revenue of £180M, 1.2 million active customers, 3.4 million orders at an average value of about £53, and a catalogue of 45,000 SKUs (Exhibits 1 and 2). It employed 620 people. Revenue had grown 14% that year, but the operating margin was only 4.1%, and the board scrutinised every overhead line. "We are a low-margin business with high expectations," Martin Hale, the chief financial officer, liked to say. "Every pound of cost has to earn its keep."

RetailCo sold only online, through its own website and app. Unlike the large marketplaces, it did not have the scale to negotiate the lowest delivery rates, and unlike the specialist boutiques, it could not charge premium prices. Its strategy depended on being reliably good: a broad range, fair prices and service that customers trusted. Exhibit 3 shows the company's organisation and Exhibit 4 the timeline of events that brought it to September 2024.

The industry

Online retail in Great Britain had settled after the pandemic surge. According to the Office for National Statistics, internet sales accounted for 19.2% of all retail sales in 2019, peaked at 30.7% in 2021, and settled at 26.6% in 2022 and 26.7% in 2023 (Exhibit 13). Growth now meant taking share from competitors rather than riding a rising tide, and customer experience was one of the few levers mid-sized retailers could pull.

Yet service quality across the UK economy was falling. The Institute of Customer Service's UK Customer Satisfaction Index stood at 75.8 out of 100 in July 2024, 0.8 points lower than a year earlier and 2.6 points below its July 2022 high. The Institute estimated that 65% of employees spent an average of 3.3 days a month dealing with service failures, at a cost to the British economy of £6.8bn a month. Customers had more choice than ever and less patience.

Into this market came generative AI. In February 2024 the payments company Klarna announced that its AI assistant had handled 2.3 million conversations in its first month, about two-thirds of its customer service chats, doing work it equated to 700 full-time agents, cutting resolution time from 11 minutes to under two, and reducing repeat enquiries by 25%, with customer satisfaction on par with human agents. A study of about 5,000 support agents by economists at Stanford and MIT, published by the US National Bureau of Economic Research in 2023, found that agents given an AI assistant resolved 13.8% more issues per hour, with the largest gains, around 35%, among the least experienced. McKinsey had estimated in 2023 that generative AI could raise productivity in customer care by 30 to 45% of current function costs (Exhibit 14).

The evidence was not all one way. In a Gartner survey of 5,728 customers published in July 2024, 64% said they would prefer that companies did not use AI for customer service, and 53% said they would consider switching to a competitor if they learned a company was going to use it. Their biggest worries were that it would become harder to reach a person, that AI would displace jobs, and that it would give wrong answers. Two widely reported incidents sharpened the last concern. In December 2023 a car dealership's website chatbot in California was coaxed by a user into "agreeing" to sell a new SUV for one dollar and calling it a "legally binding offer". And in February 2024 a Canadian tribunal ordered Air Canada to compensate a passenger after its chatbot gave him incorrect information about bereavement fares; the airline's argument that the chatbot was responsible for its own words was rejected.

The delivery problem

For most of its history RetailCo had used a single premium carrier. Its parcels arrived on time 95% of the time, and they cost an average of £4.10 each to deliver. In March 2023, with delivery costs rising and margins thin, the operations team moved to a mix of three national carriers allocated by price. The average cost per parcel fell to about £3.40, a saving the finance team estimated at £2.4M a year on 3.4 million orders.

The saving came with a price that was slower to appear. On-time delivery fell from 94% to 86% within two quarters (Exhibit 9). The cheapest of the three carriers delivered only 79% of parcels on time; its tracking data lagged by up to a day; and its API had been available 99.1% of the time in the most recent quarter, against the 99.9% promised in its contract. Customers who could not see where their parcel was contacted RetailCo instead. Contacts per order, which had been 1.1 in 2021/22, had reached 1.6 by the summer of 2024 (Exhibit 2).

In January 2024, to repair trust, RetailCo launched a Late Delivery Guarantee: a full refund for any order arriving seven or more days after its expected date, and a £5 goodwill credit for orders three to six days late (Exhibit 10). Marketing had pushed hard for it. "Customers were telling us we'd broken our promise," said Claire Dunmore, now the Chief Customer Officer. "The guarantee says we'll make it right, every time." By August, refunds under the guarantee were running at about 3,400 a month and rising, and the annualised cost of refunds and credits had reached about £2.8M (Exhibit 10). The guarantee had also created a new kind of contact: customers asking whether they qualified, and when their refund would arrive.

Customer Operations

Customer Operations occupied the second floor of the Leeds head office. It handled more than 15,000 interactions a day: about 8,000 by web chat, 4,500 by email and 2,500 by phone. Around 2,800 of these ended without reaching a person: emails closed by an automatic reply containing a tracking link, and calls resolved by the phone menu. The remaining 12,200 reached one of RetailCo's 92 agents or 12 team leaders, or the outsourced overflow team that took the excess at peak times (Exhibit 6). The fully loaded cost of an interaction handled by an agent was £0.72, and the operation cost £3.2M in 2023/24, up 22% on the year before and 49% on two years before (Exhibit 8).

Contact reasons were remarkably concentrated. Sixty-eight per cent of contacts were what the team called tier-1: "where is my order?" (31%), late-delivery refunds and refund status (17%), full-order returns (12%) and address or delivery changes (8%). Another 20% were tier-2 issues such as damaged items, product questions and payment queries, and 12% were complex cases: complaints, partial returns and possible fraud (Exhibit 5).

Agents worked in a commercial helpdesk that received email and chat and linked each ticket to a customer. To answer a typical tier-1 question, an agent opened four systems: the helpdesk; the order database, which RetailCo's engineers had built in-house on PostgreSQL; the relevant carrier's tracking site; and, for refunds, the payment provider's console. A time study in July found that 41% of the time spent on a "where is my order?" contact went on moving between those systems, and only 20% on writing to the customer (Exhibit 11). Agents could issue refunds up to £200 themselves. Larger refunds needed a team leader's approval, requested in a channel on the company's team-chat tool, which on a busy day could take hours.

The work was repetitive, and the staff survey said so. Eighty-one per cent of agents agreed that "most of my work is the same few tasks"; only 34% agreed that "I have the tools I need to do my job well"; and the employee net promoter score for the department was −22 (Exhibit 11). Annualised attrition had risen from 22% to 35% in eighteen months (Exhibit 7). Each leaver took about six weeks to replace and train, during which colleagues carried the extra volume, and response times rose further.

"I can do a WISMO in my sleep," said Tom Barker, a senior team leader who had worked on the floor for seven years. WISMO, for "where is my order?", was the team's word for its most common contact. "That's the problem. People join to help customers and spend the day copying tracking numbers between screens. The good ones leave first."

Customers noticed. Comments in the post-contact survey had grown sharper through the year (Exhibit 12). "Waited all day for a reply that just pasted the tracking link I'd already seen," wrote one. "Third time I've asked about my refund. Third different answer," wrote another.

A day on the floor

On the Thursday after the report, Priya spent a shift beside Aisha, an agent with eight months' experience. By 9:15 Aisha had three chats open and forty-one emails waiting. The first chat was a customer whose blender was four days late; Aisha checked the order, opened the carrier's site, found the parcel at a depot in Wakefield, checked the guarantee rules, added a £5 credit, and wrote a reply. It took six minutes, of which perhaps one was spent thinking about the customer. The second chat was the same question about a different parcel. The third was a customer who wanted a refund of £260 for a late sofa cushion set; Aisha posted in the approvals channel and told the customer someone would be in touch. When Priya checked at 2 p.m., the approval was still waiting.

In the afternoon Aisha took a call from an elderly customer who had been charged twice and was frightened that her card had been stolen. It took twenty minutes, and Aisha handled it with patience and skill. "That's the bit I like," she told Priya afterwards. "Actually helping someone. I just never get time for it."

An earlier attempt

RetailCo had tried automation before. In 2021 the previous head of support bought a menu-driven chatbot that asked customers to pick from buttons ("Track my order", "Returns", "Something else"). Within three months, 70% of conversations that started with the bot ended with the customer typing "agent" or choosing "Something else", and the bot was quietly switched off. The episode left a mark. "The last robot made customers angrier and gave us more work," Tom Barker told Priya. "People on the floor remember."

The options

By mid-September four options had been costed by Priya's team and the finance department, and a fifth by the engineering team (Exhibit 15).

A. Hire. Add 40 agents before the next peak. Workforce planning estimated that first response would return below two hours within about five months, at an extra £1.1M a year. Hiring did nothing about the repetitive nature of the work or the attrition it caused, and the Leeds market for experienced agents was tight.

B. Outsource tier-1. A business-process outsourcing firm offered to take all tier-1 contacts at £0.38 per interaction from an offshore centre, under a three-year contract with service-level penalties and a four-month transition. Martin Hale favoured it. "A fixed price per contact, a contract with penalties, and attrition becomes their problem, not ours," he said. Priya worried about quality, about the knowledge her best agents held, and about how hard it would be to bring the work back if it went wrong.

C. A scripted chatbot. The helpdesk vendor sold a decision-tree chatbot for £180,000 a year. It could answer fixed questions and collect order numbers, but it could not take actions such as refunds. The vendor's own reference customers reported that many conversations ended with a request for a person, and the 2021 experience made Priya sceptical.

D. Build an AI agent in-house. Daniel Reyes, the chief technology officer, proposed that a team of five engineers build an agent on Claude, Anthropic's family of large language models, using Anthropic's agent development kit and Claude Code, an AI coding assistant, to speed up the build. The agent would read a customer's message, look up the order and tracking, apply the Late Delivery Guarantee, issue refunds within limits, and pass anything else to a person with the case already summarised. Daniel estimated twelve weeks to a pilot on web chat and £350,000 for the first year of build, with a running-cost target below £0.15 per interaction (Exhibit 16). "We already own every system it needs to talk to," he said. "The hard part isn't the model. It's the controls around it, and that's engineering we know how to do."

E. Buy an AI agent platform. Two vendors offered hosted AI agents priced per resolved conversation, at £0.60 to £0.95 per resolution, with integration projects of three to six months. Both had polished demonstrations. Neither would explain in detail how their agents decided whether to issue a refund, and both wanted customer data to be processed in their own cloud environments rather than RetailCo's.

The concerns

Risk and compliance. Fiona Okafor, Head of Risk and Compliance, had circulated the Air Canada decision to the executive team with one line: "Read paragraph 27." She set out her position in a memo to Priya (Exhibit 17). "I am not against automation. I am against a system that can give away money or make promises we haven't approved, and can't tell us afterwards why it did. Under consumer law, what that bot tells a customer is what we told the customer. And under UK GDPR, I need to know where every customer's data goes and why." Her memo listed five conditions for any AI option: no customer may ever see another customer's data; refunds above £200 must still be approved by a person; every decision must be traceable after the event; customers must be told they are talking to an AI assistant; and there must be a way to switch the system off immediately.

The floor. Tom Barker raised a different concern at the monthly team meeting. "Everyone here knows what 'automation' usually means. If this is about cutting jobs, say so. If it's about taking away the work nobody wants so we can do the hard cases properly, say that, and mean it." Priya had already secured Whitfield's agreement that there would be no compulsory redundancies as a result of any change made in the next twelve months. With attrition at 35%, about 36 people would leave in a year in any case.

Finance. Martin Hale wanted payback and an exit. "Show me the payback, and show me what happens if it doesn't work. I don't want to be twelve weeks and £350,000 in with nothing to show for it." He was also uneasy about the guarantee. "We saved £2.4M on carriers and we're paying out nearly £2.8M in refunds and credits. Nobody planned it that way."

Engineering. Daniel Reyes was confident about building and candid about the gap. His team of 40 engineers had never shipped an AI system to customers. "We can build it," he said. "What I don't know yet is how we'll know it's good enough. We need to decide that before we write any code, not after."

Marketing. Claire Dunmore worried about the brand. "Sixty-four per cent of customers say they'd rather we didn't use AI. If we do it, it has to be better than what they get now, and it has to be obvious that a person is there when they need one."

The decision

Priya spent the weekend at her kitchen table with the exhibits. The numbers argued for acting quickly: another year of 22% cost growth would take the support bill to about £3.9M, and the Black Friday peak would push response times higher still. But each option carried a different kind of risk. Hiring was slow and did not change the work. Outsourcing was predictable but hard to reverse. The scripted bot was cheap but had failed before. Buying a platform was faster but opaque, and expensive per resolution. Building was the most controllable option and the least proven.

She kept returning to two lines. The first was in Exhibit 2: contacts per order had risen from 1.1 to 1.6. "A third of our volume exists because parcels are late," she wrote in her notes. "Whatever we choose, we should not just get better at apologising." The second was Fiona's question, which Priya had written on a sticky note and stuck to her laptop: How will we know, afterwards, why it did what it did?

The board paper needed four things: a recommendation, the targets by which it would be judged, the conditions under which it would be stopped, and a plan for the people on the floor. She opened a blank document and typed the title: Support 2025: Recommendation to the Board.

Assignment questions

  1. Diagnosis. What is RetailCo's real problem: cost, service quality, staff attrition or contact volume? Use Exhibits 2, 8, 9 and 10. How would your diagnosis change the recommendation?
  2. Strategy evaluation. Compare options A to E on annual cost, time to impact, risk, reversibility, control and effect on staff. Which would you recommend to the board, and why? Could options be combined?
  3. Economics. Using Exhibits 5, 6, 8 and 15, estimate the net annual benefit of an agent that handles all web-chat contacts, resolving 75% without a person, at £0.15 per interaction. At what resolution rate does it break even in year one? What assumptions matter most?
  4. The carrier decision. Using Exhibits 9 and 10, was the 2023 carrier change a good decision? What should Priya say about it in her board paper?
  5. Success. If the board approves option D, define success: five targets, each with a number, a data source, an owner and a baseline, and at least two guard-rail metrics that must not get worse.
  6. Controls. Take Fiona Okafor's five conditions (Exhibit 17). For each, describe a control that would hold even if the AI model were deceived by a malicious customer.
  7. People. What should Priya tell Tom Barker and the support team, and when? What new roles might the floor need?
  8. Stop conditions. Write the three conditions you would put in the board paper that would make RetailCo pause or reverse the rollout.

T.1Synopsis

Q5. Success. Look for number, source, owner and baseline for each target (analysis Table A9), containment defined as "resolved with no repeat contact within 48 hours", and guard rails on repeat contacts and reversed refunds.

2. Diagnosis: what is the real problem?

The board sees four symptoms: cost growing 22% a year, a 4.2-hour first reply, CSAT at 61% and attrition at 35%. Treated separately, each suggests a different fix: hire for response time, outsource for cost, automate for both. Treated as a system, they share causes.

The decisive number is in Exhibit 2. RetailCo ships about 9,315 orders a day. At 1.1 contacts per order, that would generate about 10,250 contacts; at 1.6 it generates about 14,900. Roughly 4,660 contacts a day, 31% of all volume, exist because customers are chasing parcels and refunds that the old carrier arrangement did not create.

Implication. RetailCo has a volume problem caused by operations, made worse by a work-design problem inside support. Any recommendation that answers only one of these leaves the loop running. Automation can absorb the volume and change the work; only logistics can remove the excess volume at source.

RetailCo's mix is favourable: 68% of contacts are rule-based tier-1 work. Containment, not running cost, is the variable that matters, so the pilot's first job is to measure it honestly.

11. Limits of this analysis

  • Containment is unknown until measured. Every economic conclusion rests on it; the staged pilot exists to measure it.
  • Savings arrive through attrition. With no redundancies, cost falls as about 36 people a year leave and are not replaced. The cash benefit lags the operational one.
  • The hardest contacts remain. The 25% that still reach people are longer and harder, so cost per human-handled contact will rise even as total cost falls.
  • The carrier review may change everything. If contacts per order return to 1.1, volume falls by about 31% and the scale of any automation should be revisited.
  • Customer attitudes vary. Gartner's 64% figure describes stated preferences; observed behaviour when service is fast and a person is one step away may differ. The pilot should measure it.

2.2Concept: workflow or agent? · 10 min

Say

An agent is a model that chooses its own next step: it decides which tool to call, looks at the result, and decides again, until it thinks the job is done. A workflow is a path that code fixes in advance, with a model called at some steps. Agents are flexible and expensive; workflows are cheap and predictable. Anthropic's own advice is to use the simplest thing that works. So before building any agent, ask three questions: is the path known in advance, is the task valuable enough to pay for exploration, and can mistakes be caught and undone?

Draw the spectrum on the board: single model call → workflow → workflow with a model-chosen branch → agent loop over read-only tools → agent loop with write tools. Mark where RetailCo sits: 80% workflow, 20% read-only agent loop, every write guarded in code.

Reference A · for participants and instructor

Concept cheat sheet

The thirteen layers

  1. Input · one schema for every channel; identity from the session
  2. Safety and access · intake checks + action-time guards
  3. Understanding · typed intent via forced tool call (Haiku)
  4. Memory · context, sessions, policy KB, system prompt
  5. Reasoning · SDK loop for lookups; reply from facts (Sonnet)
  6. Decision gate · rules first: plan, direct, clarify, escalate
  7. Planning · templates or model plans, validated as DAGs
  8. Task queue · parallel layers, retries, dead letter
  9. Action / tools · read, compute, write, delegate
  10. MCP · standard tool connection; in-process server
  11. Result check · completion, grounding, policy hard stops
  12. Output · tone, channel, action confirmation
  13. Monitoring and controls · traces, metrics, approvals, kill switch

Four routes

  • plan: tools needed and order id present
  • direct: policy or product question; out-of-scope deflection
  • clarify: confidence < 0.70 or order id missing
  • escalate: injection flag, fury, request for a person, failed check

Three model uses

  • Haiku: classify (Messages API, forced tool)
  • Sonnet: SDK loop; write replies; propose plans
  • Opus: judge in evaluation

Rules that never break

  1. Identity from the verified session, never from text.
  2. Ownership checked inside every tool.
  3. Every write has an idempotency key from the business action.
  4. Limits enforced twice: in the tool and in the guard.
  5. Customer agent gets no built-in tools (tools=[]).
  6. Every amount and reference grounded in tool output.
  7. Thresholds live only in config.py.

Patterns

  • Workflow vs agent: known path → workflow; open-ended → agent loop
  • Forced tool call: structured JSON every time
  • ReAct: thought → tool → observation
  • Defence in depth: screen, route, guard, check
  • Idempotency: same key → same result
  • Back-off with jitter: retry transient only
  • Circuit breaker: stop calling; serve cache
  • Bounded loop: max 3, then hand over

Timing rules

  • Running late: cut the case discussion, never the debrief.
  • Running early: run the next concept check or a mini red-team.
  • Lab 70% done at debrief time is fine; finish as homework.
  • One trio far behind: pair them with a finished trio, not with you.

Facilitation moves

  • "Show me the test." when anyone says "it works".
  • "If the prompt were deleted, what still stops it?"
  • "Which layer?" for every bug report.
  • Cold-call kindly: ask trios, not individuals.
  • Parking lot for tangents; answer next session.
  • Rotate roles daily: driver, navigator, tester.

Troubleshooting

  • claude not found: new terminal; check PATH; reinstall with the native installer.
  • Login blocked: corporate proxy; use a hotspot for first sign-in.
  • 401 from API: key not exported in this shell.
  • Hook not running: restart Claude Code after editing settings.
  • Tests hang: real back-off delays; use retry_delay=0.
  • ImportError agent: run from repo root; pip install -e .
  • Live mode in tests: conftest must set RETAILCO_OFFLINE=1 before imports.
  • Windows activate fails: Set-ExecutionPolicy -Scope CurrentUser RemoteSigned.

Contingencies

  • No internet: everything offline; demo from the reference build; skip live comparisons.
  • API outage or budget spent: stay offline; show recorded traces from your prep run.
  • Claude Code unavailable for some: they navigate for a trio that has it.
  • Mixed skill levels: strong coders take the "Going further" boxes; others run the reference build and explain it.
